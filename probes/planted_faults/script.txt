Today's chapter is "The Laws of Habit", from William James's "Talks to Teachers".
James starts with the word itself: when people say "habit", in most cases they mean a bad one.
We talk of the smoking habit and the drinking habit, but nobody talks of the courage habit.
Yet our virtues are habits just as much as our vices are.
All our life, so far as it has a definite form, is a mass of habits.
Why are we creatures of habit at all?
James believes it is because we have bodies.
The living matter of the nervous system is plastic: it grows to the way in which it has been exercised.
So we do a thing with difficulty the first time, then more and more easily, and in the end almost without thinking.
He compares this to a sheet of paper or a coat: once it is folded, it tends to fall into the same folds ever after.
Habit is a second nature, or rather, as the Duke of Wellington said, "ten times nature".
James says that ninety-nine hundredths of what we do, from rising in the morning to lying down at night, is purely automatic and habitual.
From this he draws his first conclusion for teachers: their prime concern should be to ingrain in the pupil the habits that will be most useful to him throughout life.
The great thing in all education, he says, is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy.
The more of the details of daily life we hand over to habit, the more our higher powers of mind are set free for their proper work.
The most miserable human being, he says, is the one in whom nothing is habitual but indecision, who must decide afresh when to get up and when to begin each bit of work.
Full half the time of such a man goes to deciding or regretting matters that should not exist for his consciousness at all.
James then gives a set of maxims for building a habit.
Two of them come from Professor Bain's chapter on the moral habits.
One maxim: launch yourself with as strong and decided an initiative as possible.
Take a public pledge, if the case allows, and make engagements that are incompatible with the old way.
His example is a man called Rudolph, who advertised in an Austrian paper that he would pay fifty gulden to anyone who found him at a certain wine-shop.
He did it, the advertisement said, because of a promise he had made to his wife.
Another maxim: never allow an exception until the new habit is securely rooted in your life.
Each lapse is like dropping a ball of string that you are carefully winding up: a single slip undoes more than a great many turns will wind again.
A further maxim: seize the very first possible opportunity to act on every resolution you make.
Resolves give the brain its new set not in the moment when they are formed, but in the moment when they produce action.
A fine feeling that is allowed to evaporate without bearing practical fruit is worse than a chance lost, because it hinders future resolutions from turning into action.
One maxim is for the teachers themselves: do not preach too much to your pupils, but wait for the practical opportunities and be prompt to seize them.
Here James quotes the autobiography of Darwin.
Up to the age of thirty or beyond, Darwin wrote, poetry gave him great pleasure, but for many years now he could not endure to read a line of it.
If he had to live his life again, Darwin said, he would make a rule to read some poetry and listen to some music at least once every week.
Darwin thought that the loss of these tastes was a loss of happiness, and that it may possibly be injurious to the intellect.
James concludes that an interest which is not persistently fed does not grow into a habit: it atrophies and dies.
Ten minutes a day of poetry, and an hour or two a week at music, would in due time give us all we desire, provided we began now and allowed no break.
The last maxim: keep the faculty of effort alive in you by a little gratuitous exercise every day.
Every day or two, do something for no other reason than its difficulty.
James compares this to the insurance that a man pays on his house: the payment does him no good at the time, and may never bring him a return, but if the fire does come, it saves him from ruin.
James has been accused of making old habits appear so strong that a sudden reform would be impossible.
He answers that sudden conversions, however infrequent, unquestionably do occur, and that they do not contradict his laws: new habits can be launched when there are new stimuli and new excitements.
Still, he warns that every smallest stroke of virtue or of vice leaves its scar.
Rip Van Winkle, in the play, excuses each new drink by saying "I won't count this time", but down among his nerve-cells the molecules are counting it.
James ends with the good side of this.
As we become drunkards by so many separate drinks, so we become experts by so many separate acts and hours of work.
A young person who keeps faithfully busy each hour of the working day may safely leave the final result to itself.
He will wake up some fine morning to find himself one of the competent ones of his generation.
